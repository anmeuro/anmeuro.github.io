About Houghton

Copper, snow, and the Finns who stayed — the town behind Sion's letters

Houghton sits at the northern tip of Michigan's Keweenaw Peninsula, a finger of land that juts out into Lake Superior. Its twin city, Hancock, sits directly across the water, and the two are joined by the Portage Lake Lift Bridge — the world's heaviest and widest double-deck vertical-lift bridge, and the only real way across for miles in either direction. It's a small place: about 8,000 people, built on a steep hillside overlooking the canal.

The copper that built the town

Houghton exists because of copper. In 1840, state geologist Douglass Houghton — the man the town is named after — surveyed the Keweenaw and reported rich copper deposits along Lake Superior's shore. Within a few years, prospectors and mining companies were pouring into what had been a remote, mostly unsettled corner of the state. By the 1870s, Michigan was producing more copper than any other state in the nation, and Houghton County alone supplied the majority of it. At its peak around 1910, the county's population topped 88,000 — making it one of the most populous counties in Michigan, a striking fact for a place this far north and this hard to reach.

Why the Finns came

The copper mines needed labor, and they needed it badly. Immigrants arrived from Cornwall, Italy, Germany, Ireland, Croatia, and elsewhere, but it was Finnish immigrants who left the deepest mark. The first wave came in the years after the Civil War, when experienced miners from Finland's own mining regions found their way to the Keweenaw. Word spread back home — these miners had unusually high literacy rates and wrote constantly to friends and family, describing opportunity in America in terms that triggered a genuine chain migration. By 1880 an estimated 1,500 Finns lived in the area; by 1900, roughly a quarter of Houghton County's population was Finnish-born. Entire villages in Finland emptied out toward the Copper Country.

What's still here

The mines closed decades ago — the last major one shut down in 1968 — but the Finnish imprint on this corner of Michigan never really left. Hancock is home to Finlandia University and its Finnish American Heritage Center, and the old Finnish custom of the sauna (still pronounced the traditional way here, not the Americanized one) is so embedded locally that the region calls itself part of the "Sauna Belt." Breakfast diners still serve pannukakku, a Finnish oven-baked pancake, alongside the pasty — a hand pie originally brought by Cornish miners that Finnish families adopted as their own. And scattered across the Keweenaw are the small Finnish Lutheran and Finnish Apostolic Lutheran church buildings those immigrant congregations built, more than a century ago now, many of them still standing. The building Sion's church has been restoring — the second-oldest Finnish Lutheran church in the Upper Peninsula — is one piece of that same story.

A mining school that outlasted the mines

Michigan Technological University, where Sion's campus ministry works, has its own roots in the copper boom. It was founded in 1885 as the Michigan Mining School, created specifically to train mining engineers for the local industry, and its first class of 23 students met on the second floor of the Houghton fire hall. The mines are long gone, but the school outgrew its original purpose and became the university that now anchors the town — giving Houghton, alone among the small towns of the Keweenaw, the pulse of a college town.

Winter

Houghton is, by a wide margin, the snowiest city in the Midwest, averaging around 207 inches of snow a year — enough that Michigan Tech's Winter Carnival, with its snow statue competitions, has been a campus tradition for generations. It's the kind of place where six months of snow season is simply the calendar, not the exception, which is part of what made Sion's first winter here such a story in his own newsletters.
