Acts 9:1-19 — Saul's Conversion & God's Sovereign Grace

Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me? Acts 9:4

One of the New Testament's most significant events: Saul's dramatic conversion, and what it reveals about God's sovereign grace toward sinners, spiritual transformation through humility, and the role of ordinary believers in God's mission. Two open discussions followed — why God doesn't intervene the same way for modern-day persecutors, and how parents should think about anxiety over their children's faith.

Who Saul Was Before

Saul was no neutral skeptic but an active, violent enemy of the church, "breathing threats" and extending his persecution over 200 miles from Jerusalem to Damascus. He was utterly convinced of his own righteousness, a Pharisee who considered himself above sinners and tax collectors — his pre-conversion prayer thanked God for being better than others, rather than seeking mercy — genuinely believing Jesus a false messiah and Christians all deceived.

Grace, Not Seeking

Saul wasn't searching for Jesus — Jesus came to him, a clear demonstration of grace rather than human effort, living proof of what Paul would later write in Romans: God loved us while we were still sinners. No one chooses God; God chooses us, even in our resistance — though Saul's conversion is acknowledged as unusually dramatic, not the typical pattern by which people come to faith.

Humbled Into Transformation

Once proud and self-assured, Saul was struck blind, dependent on others to lead him by the hand — his pride deliberately broken. The three days of blindness echo Christ's three days in the tomb, a kind of death before new life: the self must die before faith's new life can begin, and God uses humiliation as a necessary step in transformation. After meeting Christ, Saul's own prayer changes completely — from self-righteous declaration to humbly seeking mercy.

Jesus and the Church, United

Jesus doesn't ask "why do you persecute my people?" but "why do you persecute me?" — Christ identifies himself directly with the church; attacking Christians is attacking Christ himself.

Ananias: an Ordinary Believer

Ananias is simply called "a disciple" — not an apostle, not a famous leader. Despite Saul becoming one of the New Testament's most consequential figures, God chose an unknown, ordinary person as the instrument of his restoration: God doesn't use only the powerful or famous — any obedient believer can be used. And Ananias's obedience wasn't blind — he honestly told God his fear of Saul's dangerous reputation. Faith isn't the absence of fear; it's obeying God in spite of it, and every believer can bring honest fear to God rather than hiding it. God prepared both men simultaneously — giving Saul a vision of Ananias coming to heal him, even as he sent Ananias on his way.

God's Chosen Instrument

God calls Saul "my chosen instrument" — a choice made before his transformation, even while he was persecuting the church. Saul's own qualifications — deep scriptural knowledge, Jewish heritage, Roman citizenship, education, and brilliance — made him well suited; God didn't create new qualities in him, only redirected the ones already there toward reaching Gentiles, kings, and Israel. God also foretold Saul would suffer greatly for his name — imprisonment, beatings, stoning, rejection — directly against any assumption that God's call guarantees comfort.

Brotherhood and Baptism

Ananias greets Saul as "Brother Saul" — immediately accepting a former enemy as a fellow believer, the gospel's power of reconciliation extending not only between people and God but between people themselves. The scales falling from Saul's eyes carry double meaning: his restored physical sight mirrors his spiritual eyes opening — as in "Amazing Grace," "I once was blind, but now I see," an experience shared by every believer, not Saul alone. His baptism publicly identified him with Christ's death, burial, and resurrection, completing his conversion.

Why Doesn't God Do This Today?

If God changed Saul mid-persecution, why doesn't he do the same for those persecuting Christians today, in China, Ethiopia, or the Middle East? God does still change persecutors — many like Saul come to faith today, officials and Muslims among them, just through less dramatic means. Saul's own dramatic conversion answered a unique historical need: the New Testament didn't yet exist, the church had no settled doctrine, and depended entirely on apostolic teaching and authority — losing an apostle then carried catastrophic weight for the whole church in a way it simply doesn't now, with a complete Bible and global Christian resources available to every believer.

When Children's Faith Falters

Drawing on Old Testament examples — Aaron's sons, David and Solomon — one participant voiced real anxiety about college-aged children who attended church as kids but are now skeptical. The response: faith is personal, not inherited — Old Testament history repeatedly shows faith failing to pass down through family lines; each person must build their own direct relationship with God. Parents must first be good examples themselves, since children's struggles often trace back to parental behavior or relational patterns — faith lived only "vertically," with God, but not "horizontally," with the people around us, is incomplete. Love children unconditionally, not according to their performance or belief — judging them by behavior damages the relationship, while feeling genuinely honored by parents naturally draws children toward honoring their parents' faith over time. It's never too late — Paul himself was a grown adult, deeply set in opposition to God, when he was transformed — and children leaving the faith in college is often part of building independence, where maintaining relationship matters more than control. Parents should do their best, model faith well, and trust God with the outcome rather than carrying the weight themselves.
