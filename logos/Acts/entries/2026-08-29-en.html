Acts 11:1–30 — Peter's Defense to Jerusalem and the Church at Antioch

Do not call anything impure that God has made clean. Acts 11:9

This session worked through Acts 11, tracing Peter's explanation to the Jerusalem church of his visit to the Gentile household of Cornelius, and then following the gospel as persecution pushed it outward from Jerusalem into Antioch. The group discussed Jewish cultural prejudice against Gentiles, God's threefold vision correcting Peter, the discipleship partnership of Barnabas and Saul, and where the name "Christian" actually came from. The session ran bilingually in Chinese and English, with LJM as the main teacher, iPhone translating and adding commentary in Chinese, and Dr contributing through questions.

Peter Explains Himself to Jerusalem

In chapter 10, Peter had gone to the house of Cornelius, a Gentile centurion, and Cornelius's whole household received the Spirit and was saved — a serious breach of Jewish custom, since Jews did not enter Gentile homes, let alone eat at their table. When Peter returned to Jerusalem, some "circumcised believers" criticized him for entering an uncircumcised man's house and eating with them. The group noted that this objection came from accumulated ethnic prejudice and cultural distance, not from the law of God itself: from the time of Moses through David, Jews had built strict boundaries around marriage and social contact with outsiders, and those boundaries had hardened into discrimination. What happened at Cornelius's house was therefore "epoch-making" — the Spirit falling on Gentiles meant salvation had broken through ethnic lines, a turning point in God's plan to reach all humanity.

Three Visions: Dismantling Peter's Prejudice

While praying in Joppa, Peter saw a large sheet lowered from heaven holding both clean and unclean animals, with a voice telling him to "get up, kill and eat." He refused, protesting that he had never eaten anything impure. The voice answered in verse 9 — the passage's central theological statement — "Do not call anything impure that God has made clean." Its point isn't really about food; it's God declaring that Gentiles have been accepted, and that no one may call "unclean" what God has set apart as holy. The group noted that "three times" in Jewish culture signals certainty and completeness, much like "seven" — this was no accident, but God's unmistakable will, driven home as many times as it took to break through Peter's deep-seated bias. Peter didn't grasp it all at once either; his understanding moved from confusion on the rooftop to real comprehension only once he watched the Spirit fall on Gentiles with his own eyes. Leviticus had drawn clear lines between clean and unclean animals (pork, for instance, unclean because it doesn't chew the cud), and Jewish tradition had piled more than six thousand additional regulations on top of that — Peter's resistance was this whole legalistic weight showing itself in the moment.

A Universal Reflection on Prejudice

LJM asked the group to turn this inward: who are the "Gentiles" or the "unclean" in our own cultural blind spots? It's the same question the Pharisees put to Jesus — "who is my neighbor?" — answered with the parable of the Good Samaritan, where the Samaritan was precisely the untouchable figure in Jewish eyes. The translator offered a concrete Chinese example: Han Christians in Yinchuan, Ningxia, sometimes assume Hui people (especially from places like Zhongning or Tongxin) "could never believe" — exactly the kind of prejudgment God has to correct. The group's principle: everyone deserves to hear the gospel, whether ordinary, elite, or imprisoned, and no one should become an obstacle to God's redemptive plan. And this correction isn't only for outsiders — even genuinely born-again believers may still need God to keep correcting cultural prejudice and inherited tradition.

Peter's Humility and Strategy as a Witness

Peter already carried real authority in the early church — he had preached at Pentecost and repeatedly taken a central role in Acts. He could simply have declared that Gentiles may be baptized and left it at that. Instead he patiently walked the Jerusalem believers through what had happened, step by step — genuine humility rather than a use of power. Old Testament law required only two witnesses, but Peter had brought six brothers with him to Cornelius's house; the group read that as deliberate over-provision, since the event was so hard to believe that his word alone might not have carried it. Peter held authority but leaned on others' testimony instead of demanding acceptance — a model, the group said, for leading by persuasion rather than by position.

Cornelius: A Good Man Who Still Needed Salvation

Cornelius was a wealthy Roman centurion whom Scripture calls devout, God-fearing, generous, and constant in prayer — by every visible measure, someone sincerely seeking God. Precisely because of that hunger and character, God sent an angel to direct him to Peter, which the group took as evidence that God examines the heart and knows when someone is ready to receive salvation. But the key theological point was sharper: a prayer life and good deeds were "good conditions," not sufficient ones — religious background, moral conduct, and social standing cannot substitute for personally trusting Christ. That applies as much to the ordinary person ("I haven't killed or burned anything down") as to philosophers, rabbis, or scholars of philosophy and Scripture. The translator extended the same principle into Chinese cultural terms: figures revered as sages, like Laozi and Zhuangzi, and sincere Buddhist believers, are no exception — moral behavior and philosophical wisdom cannot stand in for personal faith in Christ.

The Gospel Moves from Jerusalem to Antioch

Persecution after Stephen's martyrdom scattered the disciples out of Jerusalem — on the surface a disaster, but actually the means God used to push the gospel outward. "God can make bad things accomplish good purposes." LJM walked through the expansion in three stages:

  • Jerusalem and its surroundings — a purely Jewish region.
  • Phoenicia, Cyprus, Sidon, and similar places — mixed Jewish and Gentile territory, comparable to Samaria's blended culture.
  • Antioch — a fully Gentile, Greek-speaking world, marking the gospel's complete breakthrough of ethnic boundaries.

Antioch became the first genuinely Gentile church and later the base for Paul's missionary journeys; it's also where the name "Christian" was first used.

On the map: Tarsus, Paul's hometown, sits on the right; Cyrene, in the lower left of North Africa, was home to Simon, who was compelled to carry Jesus's cross; and Cyprus, the island at the map's center, was Barnabas's home.

Barnabas: A Model Leader and Paul's Advocate

Verse 24 calls Barnabas "a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and faith" — his name means "son of encouragement," and his character and gifting made him the obvious choice to send to Antioch. As the Gentile church there kept growing, Barnabas recognized that Saul's gifts and calling were needed and made a special trip to Tarsus to bring him in as a co-worker. Saul was, at that point, still on the margins of the believing community — he had explained himself to the apostles after his conversion, but many still distrusted a former persecutor of Christians, and Barnabas was one of the few who fully trusted him. The group drew out Barnabas's leadership qualities: he could recognize someone else's gifts without jealousy or competition, he was willing to fully back a person others still doubted, and he had earlier served as the bridge introducing Saul to the Jerusalem apostles. Saul's fluency in Greek, Hebrew, and his native Tarsus dialect made him especially suited to ministry in that multicultural Gentile world.

Evangelism and Discipleship, Together

Barnabas and Saul spent a full year in Antioch teaching the church — new believers needed more than the gospel message itself; they needed to systematically learn who Jesus is, what the gospel means, and how to actually live as Christians. The group's core principle: evangelism and discipleship have to go together — evangelism brings people to Christ, discipleship helps them grow in him, and neither one alone is enough. The translator illustrated this with a Chinese proverb roughly equivalent to "give a man a fish" — leading someone to faith is only walking them to the edge of the pond; they still need to be taught how to fish, meaning ongoing discipleship and spiritual guidance. The young Antioch church faced very practical questions as a result — whether food offered to idols could be eaten, how the church should be run, how to care for members in need — all of which needed time and teaching to work through.

Where the Name "Christian" Came From

Antioch is where believers were first called "Christians" — the first time the term appears in history. Literally it means "followers of Christ" or "those who imitate Christ," covering the whole of Jesus's love, conduct, and values rather than serving as a bare religious label. At the time it wasn't neutral at all — it carried mockery and insult, since Jesus had died by crucifixion and was widely regarded as a criminal or an accursed man; calling someone a "Christian" was effectively calling them a follower of a condemned criminal. Peter's own words in 1 Peter 4:16, telling believers not to be ashamed when insulted by that name, confirm that the term really was used to shame people. Today "Christian" is neutral or even positive, though the translator noted that many believers now prefer "follower of Jesus" as a fuller expression of what the identity actually means.
