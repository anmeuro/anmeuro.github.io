Acts 14:1-20 — Iconium, Lystra, and Derbe: Miracles, Idolatry, and Paul Stoned

Turn from these worthless things to the living God. Acts 14:15

This session examined Acts 14, tracing Paul and Barnabas's first missionary journey through Iconium, Lystra, and Derbe. The discussion highlighted a recurring pattern: the gospel is proclaimed, some believe, and others oppose fiercely. Key themes included the purpose of miracles (to confirm the message, not to glorify the messenger), the danger of idolatry even among believers, and the need to contextualize evangelism for different audiences. The session closed with a Q&A on the relationship between Jewish and Christian faith and the nature of the Messiah.

Orienting by the Map

The map of the first missionary journey served as a visual reference for the geographical progression: Antioch (Syria) → Cyprus → Pisidian Antioch → Iconium → Lystra → Derbe, and then back to Antioch.

Ministry in Iconium (Acts 14:1–6)

Paul and Barnabas preached in the Jewish synagogue, and many Jews and Greeks believed. Unbelieving Jews stirred up the Gentiles against them, intensifying the opposition. The apostles nevertheless remained for a long time, preaching boldly, and God confirmed their message through signs and wonders. The city divided into two camps — those who sided with the apostles and those who sided with the Jewish opponents. When a plot to stone them was discovered, they fled to Lystra and Derbe.

Healing in Lystra and the Crowd's Reaction (Acts 14:7–13)

Paul healed a man who had been lame from birth. The crowd responded by calling Barnabas "Zeus" and Paul "Hermes" — because Paul was the chief speaker. A priest of Zeus brought oxen and garlands to the city gates to offer sacrifice to them.

Paul and Barnabas Reject Worship; Paul Is Stoned (Acts 14:14–20)

Paul and Barnabas tore their clothes and rushed into the crowd, firmly refusing worship and redirecting attention to the living God. Paul preached about God's common grace — rain, seasons, and food — as evidence of the one true Creator. Then Jews from Antioch and Iconium arrived and incited the crowd; Paul was stoned and dragged outside the city, presumed dead. He rose, re-entered the city, and the next day departed with Barnabas for Derbe.

The Repeated Pattern of Mission

A consistent cycle runs through Paul's journeys: preach → some believe → opposition rises → persecution → move to the next city. The same message produces two different responses — belief and rejection — and this duality is expected, not exceptional. It mirrors Jesus's own teaching to the Twelve: when persecuted in one city, move to another; the priority is gospel proclamation, not confrontation or debate. The disciples' movement from city to city was not driven by fear but by strategic focus — bringing the gospel to those who had not yet heard it.

Miracles: Confirmation, Not Celebrity

God confirmed the apostles' message through signs and wonders, particularly during the early period of gospel expansion. The healing of the lame man in Lystra is explicitly parallel to Peter's healing at the Beautiful Gate — both men were lame from birth. Miracles are meant to authenticate the message about Jesus Christ as Lord, not to elevate the status of the preacher. Paul and Barnabas modeled the correct response: when the crowd tried to worship them, they forcefully rejected it, tearing their garments — a Jewish expression of horror at blasphemy.

A key warning followed: miracles alone do not lead people to the true God. The Lystra crowd interpreted the healing through their existing pagan framework and worshipped the wrong beings entirely.

The Danger of Idolatry and Misinterpreting God

People interpret extraordinary events through the assumptions they already hold; the Lystra crowd defaulted to their Greek/Roman polytheistic worldview. This is not unique to Gentiles — the Israelites made the golden calf while Moses was on Sinai, attributing their deliverance from Egypt to an idol. Christians are equally susceptible: without consistent engagement with Scripture, one's mental image of God can become distorted or syncretized with other belief systems. The group noted how a believer's faith can become confused by mixing astrology, Buddhism, and Christianity — illustrating the real risk of an unbiblical image of God.

Leadership and Redirecting Glory to God

Paul and Barnabas set the model for Christian leaders: when ministry succeeds, redirect all attention and worship toward God, not toward themselves. Spiritual leaders face a particular temptation to "steal God's glory" — whether by enjoying praise for sermons or by feeling deflated when there is no response. The correct posture is to faithfully fulfill the duty of proclamation; the work of transformation in people's hearts belongs to God. If someone begins to elevate or worship a leader, it must be firmly and immediately redirected.

Contextualizing the Gospel

Paul's approach to preaching differed significantly depending on his audience:

  • To Jews: he argued from Old Testament history, the patriarchs, David, and messianic prophecy, since they shared scriptural foundations.
  • To Gentile pagans: he did not use Scripture (unknown to them) but appealed to common grace — rain, harvests, food, seasons — as evidence of the one Creator God.

With polytheists, the evangelistic sequence required first moving them from many gods to one God, and then introducing the person and work of Jesus Christ. The principle applies broadly: in intellectually sophisticated cities, rational dialogue and Bible study are effective; in communities with less formal education (for example, parts of Africa), signs and wonders may play a larger role. Paul in Athens, later in Acts, was also referenced — he used the inscription "To an Unknown God" as an entry point for proclamation. Effective evangelism requires knowing the person, understanding their "pain points," praying for guidance, and adapting the method accordingly.

Creation as Revelation

Paul's appeal to common grace reflects a broader biblical principle: creation reveals God's invisible attributes — his power, wisdom, and divine nature. This is echoed in Romans: through what has been made, people can perceive the Creator's glory, yet they often give that glory to created things instead. From the microscopic (cells, DNA, atoms) to the cosmic (galaxies, the universe), scientific discovery increasingly points to the magnitude of God's creative power. A story was shared of a geologist who came to faith after examining mineral structures under a magnifying glass and being overwhelmed by the intricacy of God's creation. Astronauts who have seen Earth from space were cited as examples of people moved to worship by the perspective of the cosmos.

Paul's Suffering and Bodily Marks

The stoning at Lystra was likely the most life-threatening event of Paul's entire ministry. His injuries were so severe that the crowd believed he was dead. In Galatians 6:17, Paul later wrote, "I bear on my body the marks of Jesus" — possibly referring to permanent physical scars or disabilities from this event. Paul's physical appearance was reportedly unimpressive, and some speculate a disability resulting from this stoning. The discussion also connected the event to 2 Corinthians 12, where Paul describes being "caught up to the third heaven" and not knowing whether he was in or out of the body — possibly a near-death experience during this stoning.

There is a further layer of irony: Paul himself had previously approved the stoning of Stephen (Acts 7), and now he suffered the same fate, deepening his understanding of his own past sin and of the grace he had received.

The Instability of Human Nature

The same crowd that wanted to worship Paul and Barnabas as gods joined in stoning Paul shortly afterward, incited by Jews from Antioch and Iconium. This mirrors the Palm Sunday crowd that welcomed Jesus as king and within days called for his crucifixion. The lesson: human beings are unreliable; only God is constant. Without the Holy Spirit's ongoing work, any person is capable of dramatic moral reversal.

  • Reflection: When my service is praised — or ignored — where does my heart go? Do I redirect the glory to God?
  • Reflection: What assumptions do I bring to my picture of God? Am I shaping it by Scripture, or by other beliefs?
  • Practice: Before sharing the gospel with someone, learn who they are and what they carry, and pray for wisdom to speak in a way they can understand.
