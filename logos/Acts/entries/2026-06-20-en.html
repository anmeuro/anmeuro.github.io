Acts 5:33-6:15 — Gamaliel's Counsel, Seven Deacons & Stephen

The apostles left the Sanhedrin, rejoicing because they had been counted worthy of suffering disgrace for the Name. Acts 5:41

This session covered Acts 5:33-42 and all of chapter 6: the Sanhedrin's hostile response to the apostles, Gamaliel's moderating counsel, the apostles' joyful response to flogging, the appointment of seven deacons to resolve internal tension, and an introduction to Stephen's ministry and arrest.

The Sanhedrin's Fury and Gamaliel's Warning

Hearing Peter's testimony — that the Jesus they condemned had been raised by God, vindicated as righteous, now King and Savior and the source of repentance and forgiveness — the Sanhedrin wanted the apostles put to death, echoing their treatment of Jesus himself. The same message that led about 3,000 ordinary people to repent and be baptized at Pentecost met only hostility here — even though these leaders had more evidence than ordinary people did (an empty tomb, escaped prisoners, miracles), showing that unbelief is not a lack of evidence but a hardness of heart.

Gamaliel — a respected Pharisee, and notably Paul's own teacher — urged caution, citing past failed movements (Theudas with some 400 followers, Judas the Galilean) that collapsed once their leaders were killed: if this movement is merely human, it will fade on its own; if it is from God, opposing it means opposing God. Gamaliel's principle isn't universally reliable — some human movements do persist a long time — but it correctly recognizes that human authority cannot defeat God's purposes.

Flogging, Release, and Continued Preaching

The apostles were flogged — a severe Roman-style beating, the same punishment Paul later received 39 times — ordered never to speak in Jesus' name again, and released. Their response was joy, not from personal strength but because suffering for Jesus' name identified them with Christ — a supernatural joy given by the Spirit. This fulfilled Jesus' own words in Matthew 5:11-12, which the disciples hadn't understood at the time he spoke them, but did once empowered by the Spirit after Pentecost. And so they never stopped teaching and proclaiming Jesus as Messiah, in the temple courts and house to house — eyewitnesses to his life, death, resurrection, and ascension, and witnesses to the Spirit's power.

Internal Conflict and Seven Deacons

Up to this point the church faced external persecution; now it faces internal conflict — a pattern recognized as Satan's other typical line of attack. Two groups made up the Jerusalem church: Hebrew Jews, native to Judea, Aramaic-speaking; and Hellenistic Jews, from the wider diaspora, Greek-speaking and shaped by Greco-Roman culture — cultural and linguistic differences that created real friction, not unlike tensions today between, say, Chinese Christians raised in the West and those from mainland China.

The presenting problem: Hellenistic widows were being overlooked in the daily food distribution — likely poor administration amid rapid growth rather than deliberate discrimination. In Jewish tradition, caring for widows, orphans, and strangers is a divine command; God identifies himself especially as their God, and these Christian widows, having converted, were likely excluded from temple relief programs, making the church's own provision essential.

The apostles, recognizing they couldn't manage both spiritual ministry and daily administration, had the church select seven men of good reputation, full of the Spirit and wisdom — Stephen (the most prominent), Philip, Procorus, Nicanor, Timon, Parmenas, and Nicolas of Antioch. This parallels Moses in the wilderness, delegating to elders on his father-in-law's advice so he could focus on his own priorities. No ministry in the church is higher or lower than another — preaching, prayer, food distribution, ushering, even parking-lot duty are all Spirit-empowered ministry, assigned according to calling and gifting. The result: the word of God spread, the number of disciples in Jerusalem grew greatly, and even a large number of priests became obedient to the faith.

Stephen's Ministry and Arrest

Stephen, appointed for practical service, was also full of grace and power, performing great wonders among the people — showing that every role in the church is Spirit-empowered. Opposition arose from members of the Synagogue of the Freedmen (formerly enslaved Jews from Cyrene, Alexandria, Cilicia, and Asia). Unable to defeat Stephen's wisdom and the Spirit in debate, they resorted to false charges: blasphemy against the temple, since Stephen had taught — echoing Jesus' own teaching — that the temple had become corrupted, a marketplace and den of thieves, and that empty ritual is no substitute for God's presence; and blasphemy against the Law of Moses, since Stephen taught that Jesus had fulfilled and completed it, inaugurating a new covenant. Stephen was arrested and brought before the Sanhedrin — and those looking at him saw his face like the face of an angel, a supernatural peace and confidence given by God, echoing the apostles' own composure under trial.

  • Theme: the same truth produces opposite reactions depending on the condition of the heart — unbelief is a spiritual problem, not an evidential one.
  • Theme: persecution does not diminish a believer's joy; suffering for Jesus' name is itself a mark of identification with him.
  • Theme: "we must obey God rather than human beings" remains the passage's central ethical stance.
  • Theme: Satan's strategy against the church runs on two fronts — external persecution and internal division.
