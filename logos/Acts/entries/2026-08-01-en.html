Acts 9:20-43 — Saul's Escape, Aeneas & Dorcas Raised

This Jesus is indeed the Son of God. Acts 9:20

Saul's preaching immediately after his conversion, the persecution and escapes that followed in Damascus and Jerusalem, and Peter's two miracles — healing Aeneas and raising Dorcas. The core thread: true conversion always brings complete life transformation and immediate action, with Saul's own transformation the most dramatic example, while Peter's miracles focus not on themselves but on leading people to faith — and quietly foreshadow his preaching to the Gentiles in chapter 10.

Saul Preaches Immediately

Scripture records Saul proclaiming Jesus as the Son of God in the synagogues immediately after his conversion, without waiting to study or prepare — that immediacy itself is a mark of genuine conversion, since only real transformation produces that irrepressible urge to proclaim. His preaching centered on Jesus as the Son of God and promised Messiah, using Old Testament Scripture as proof, leaving listeners amazed.

Same Zeal, New Direction

Before conversion Saul zealously persecuted Christians; afterward he proclaimed Jesus with the same zeal and strength. God didn't change his character, only its direction — his earnestness and wholehearted commitment stayed exactly as they were, simply redirected. His training under Gamaliel, once used against the church, now became a tool for it — able to connect Old Testament prophecy directly to Jesus with real persuasive force (most of the New Testament's epistles, outside the Gospels, were in fact written by him).

Persecution, Escape, and a Lesson in Humility

After roughly three years preaching in Damascus, Saul faced threats from both Jewish and civil authorities at once — but Jesus had already warned him at his conversion that he would suffer for his name, so none of it came as a surprise. Disciples lowered him over the city wall in a basket at night — an extremely humbling way to leave, not a dignified exit. Despite his noble birth, education, and status, God used this humiliating escape to shape his character. Paul later boasts in 2 Corinthians not of his background but of this very weakness, saying "in my weakness, Christ's strength is displayed."

Barnabas's Crucial Role

Returning to Jerusalem, Saul tried to join the disciples, but his reputation as a persecutor made everyone afraid — no one would receive him. Only Barnabas ("son of encouragement") stepped in, testifying before the apostles to Saul's encounter on the Damascus road and his three years of preaching, which finally let Saul be accepted. When Saul then debated Hellenistic Jews in Jerusalem, they quickly sought to kill him — a striking reversal, since Saul had once stood with people like these against Stephen, and now stood in Stephen's place himself. Learning of the plot, the brothers escorted him to Caesarea and on to Tarsus — Saul learning, in the process, to accept help rather than relying only on his own strength.

Quiet Preparation, and Peace for the Church

Tarsus was Saul's hometown; Scripture doesn't detail what he did there, but presumably prayer, meditation, and study preparing him for his later mission to the Gentiles. With Saul gone from Jerusalem, the churches throughout Judea, Galilee, and Samaria enjoyed a season of peace and growth — precisely because their chief persecutor had been converted. It was Barnabas again who later went to find Saul in Tarsus and brought him to Antioch, launching his larger ministry.

Peter Heals Aeneas

Peter meets Aeneas in Lydda, paralyzed and bedridden for eight years, and declares, "Jesus Christ heals you, get up" — not "I heal you." The focus stays on Jesus' name and power, not Peter's own. Everyone in Lydda and Sharon who saw it turned to the Lord — the miracle's purpose was always to lead people to faith, never the miracle itself.

Peter Raises Dorcas

Dorcas ("gazelle"), a disciple in Joppa known for always doing good and helping the poor — a quiet, faithful example of discipleship without any position of leadership — falls ill and dies. Peter sends everyone out, kneels to pray, calls her name, and she is raised — closely mirroring how Jesus raised Jairus's daughter, Peter deliberately following that same pattern, fulfilling Jesus' own promise that believers would do the works he did, and even greater things. News spread through all Joppa, and many believed — again, the point was always the people brought to faith, not the miracle in itself.

A Quiet Foreshadowing

Peter's choice to stay with Simon the tanner in Joppa seems like a small detail but is not — a tanner's work, handling animal hides and blood, was considered unclean under Jewish law. This was the start of God gradually breaking down Peter's traditional categories, preparing him to enter a Gentile household in chapter 10, where associating with Gentiles was likewise considered unclean. Before sending Peter into that Gentile home, God first had him stay in an "unclean" household, then used the vision in chapter 10 to teach him, step by step, "what God has made clean, you must not call unclean."

Why Fewer Miracles Today?

Without a completed New Testament, the early church relied heavily on oral proclamation, and God used miracles and signs to reveal himself more directly as the church was being established. God still works according to circumstance and need — fewer recorded miracles in highly secularized regions, more common testimony in places more attuned to the spiritual realm — and personal miracles, less dramatic but still contrary to nature, continue today, just under different names. Miracles themselves cannot change a heart: Jesus rebuked those who followed him only for the signs, and Israel's own history — the Red Sea, manna in the wilderness — proves that even great miracles don't produce real faith on their own. The greatest miracle remains the transformation of an entire belief system, from unbelief to faith in Christ — miracles are simply tools God uses to reveal himself; faith itself has to rest on God's Word, not on the miracle.
