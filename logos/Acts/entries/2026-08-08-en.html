Acts 10:1-23 — Cornelius and Peter: Two Parallel Transformations

Do not call anything impure that God has made clean. Acts 10:15

Acts 10 marks a pivotal turning point — God's deliberate opening of the gospel to the Gentiles, worked out through two parallel transformations, one in Cornelius and one in Peter. This session covered verses 1–23, examining how God prepared both men at the same time through visions and instruction, drawing two lessons: the need to break through personal and cultural boundaries, and the call to obey God even without full understanding.

Cornelius: A Devout Man

Cornelius was a Roman centurion — a man of social rank, means, and genuine piety, described as devout, God-fearing, generous to the poor, and consistent in prayer, unlike the hypocrisy of some religious leaders. This raises a real question: does a morally upright person still need the gospel? Like the Rich Young Ruler, who kept every law yet still lacked something essential — or figures like Confucius or Laozi, admired for their virtue, who still need to encounter Christ. God sees the heart, not outward behavior; no one meets the standard "be perfect as your Father in heaven is perfect." Every world religion apart from Christianity teaches salvation by works; Scripture teaches salvation by grace through faith — everyone falls short of God's glory, even the most virtuous person carrying some hidden hatred, jealousy, fear, or resentment.

Peter's Vision

Praying on a rooftop at noon, hungry, Peter fell into a trance and saw a large sheet lowered from heaven holding all kinds of animals, including those forbidden under Mosaic law. A voice told him, "Get up, Peter. Kill and eat" — and he refused three times, citing the Law. This wasn't rebellion but genuine confusion, a lifetime of observance making the command feel incomprehensible. The key verse, "Do not call anything impure that God has made clean," applies to people, not only food — God was declaring Gentiles, cleansed through Christ, no longer "common" or unclean, echoing Jesus' own teaching that defilement comes from the heart, not what enters the mouth. The vision repeats three times — both emphasis and a mirror of Peter's own life, where he denied Jesus three times and was later asked three times, "Do you love me?" — a recurring pattern of threefold restoration in Peter's story.

The Servants Arrive

While Peter was still puzzling over the vision, Cornelius's servants arrived at the gate — a remarkably timed convergence of God's two parallel workings. The Spirit told Peter directly: "Three men are looking for you... I have sent them." Hearing about Cornelius's vision and character, Peter invited the men in as guests — itself a significant crossing of a cultural line, since Jewish custom forbade entering Gentile homes or sharing meals with them. Hosting Gentile strangers, in a house where he himself was a guest, shows his transformation had already begun. He set out with them the next day, accompanied by several brothers from Joppa.

Why People, Not Angels

God could have sent an angel to preach directly to Cornelius but chose not to — his pattern is co-working with people, inviting them into his mission not merely for efficiency but to transform the participants themselves. Paul called himself God's "co-worker"; believers are called as witnesses — to say who Jesus is, preach the gospel, make disciples, teach Scripture. Using Peter here served two purposes at once: reaching Cornelius, and deepening Peter's own transformation.

God's Original Heart for the Nations

God's love for all nations goes back to the beginning, not a New Testament innovation — in Moses' day, Israel was called to be a kingdom of priests, a nation mediating between God and the Gentile world, a channel of blessing to all peoples. Israel failed that calling: Jewish tradition built walls of hostility toward Gentiles, treating them as unclean — the opposite of what God intended. That human tradition, not God's own heart, is the barrier Peter had to overcome.

Two Lessons

First, breaking through personal and cultural boundaries: everyone operates inside boundaries shaped by nationality, culture, ethnicity, personality, family, and class — educated families looking down on blue-collar workers, social circles that only mix within their own layer. God calls believers to break through these, loving and connecting across class and culture, as Peter was called to do. Second, obeying God without full understanding: Peter acted before he fully understood or accepted the command — resistance and obedience together, unlike Jonah, who understood God's will and simply refused it. The call is to trust that God's purposes exceed our own understanding — "my ways are higher than your ways" — obedience rooted in relationship and trust, even amid real uncertainty.
