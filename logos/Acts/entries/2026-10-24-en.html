Acts 13:13-52 — Paul's Sermon in Pisidian Antioch: Resurrection, Forgiveness, and Justification

Through him everyone who believes is justified from everything the law of Moses could not justify. Acts 13:39

The group completed Acts 13, focusing on Paul and Barnabas's first missionary journey and Paul's sermon in Pisidian Antioch. The central themes were the continuity of the gospel with Old Testament prophecy, the resurrection of Jesus as the cornerstone of Christian faith, and the twin gifts of forgiveness and justification available only through Christ. Two clarifying questions were also addressed: the nature and timing of resurrection (and how it differs from resuscitation), and the translation of Acts 13:38–39 on justification under Moses' law versus faith in Christ.

Structural Transition in Acts

The mission base shifted from Jerusalem to Antioch — a predominantly Gentile city — a major transition in the book. The primary missionary figure shifted from Peter to Paul, who was specifically called as an apostle to the Gentiles. Paul and Barnabas departed from Syrian Antioch, traveled through Cyprus, and then moved north to the mainland; this session picked up from Cyprus onward, following the remaining cities marked on the route map from the previous session. Paul's preaching strategy throughout the chapter is to present the gospel not as a new religion, but as the fulfillment of existing Old Testament prophecy — connecting the same God of Israel to Jesus the Messiah.

Setting and Audience (vv. 13–16)

On the Sabbath, Paul and Barnabas entered the synagogue in Pisidian Antioch — a different city from their starting point, though it shares the same name. The synagogue audience included both Jewish people and Gentiles, reflecting Paul's universal mandate. After the public reading of the Law and the Prophets (likely read in large sections rather than a few selected verses), the synagogue leader invited the visitors to share an encouraging word — and Paul stood up voluntarily.

A Summary of Israel's History (vv. 17–25)

Paul began by recapping God's faithfulness: choosing the ancestors; multiplying Israel from about 70 people to roughly two million during the slavery in Egypt; leading them through the wilderness with patience despite repeated disobedience; and granting them the Promised Land. He then traced the line through King Saul (who was removed) to King David — a foreshadowing of the coming Messiah — and directly on to Jesus, skipping the intervening history to highlight only the key figures.

Paul identified John the Baptist as the final preparatory prophet, arriving after a long period of divine silence following Malachi; his role was to preach repentance and point to the coming Messiah. The intent of the whole survey: to show that the gospel is not a new religion but the culmination of God's ancient promises — the same God who led Israel is the God who sent Jesus.

Jesus as the Fulfillment of Scripture (vv. 26–37)

In Luke 24, Jesus himself said that all of Scripture — the Law, the Prophets, and the Psalms — is written about him and must be fulfilled through him; Paul's preaching embodies exactly this approach. The rejection and crucifixion of Jesus by Jerusalem's leaders was not accidental: it fulfilled specific Old Testament prophecy, making Jesus not an incidental victim but the intentional sacrificial fulfillment. In verses 33–35 Paul cites three Old Testament passages to support Jesus's resurrection, his identity as the Son of God, and the fulfillment of God's promise to David.

The Resurrection as the Core of the Gospel

Paul's central declaration is, "I bring you good news" — the promised Messiah is Jesus, and God raised him from the dead. The resurrection is God's confirmation that Jesus is the promised Savior and that his atoning work cannot be defeated by death. Paul argues in 1 Corinthians that if Christ is not resurrected, Christians are "the most miserable of all people" — their sacrifice of time, obedience, and suffering would be meaningless. The resurrection also establishes our own future hope: Jesus is the "firstfruits" — the prototype — and believers will likewise be raised.

Forgiveness and Justification (vv. 38–39)

Two essential gifts are found only in Christ:

  • Forgiveness of sins — without it, condemnation stands.
  • Justification — being declared righteous before God, which makes reconciliation and eternal life possible.

The law of Moses can only demonstrate guilt, proving one's unrighteousness; it cannot justify. Three principles are embedded in verse 39:

  • Only through Jesus — he is the exclusive way, truth, and life.
  • For everyone — the scope is universal, not limited to Jews; all ethnicities and religious backgrounds are included.
  • Through faith alone — not by behavior, merit, law-keeping, or good works, but by grace through faith.

Justification means that God does not count sin against the believer — the sin is transferred to Christ. This is the very definition of grace: unearned, not a reward for deeds.

Warning and Call to Decision (vv. 40–41)

Paul quoted Habakkuk as a warning: hearing the gospel is not enough — every person must decide whether to believe or reject. The choice carries eternal consequences: belief leads to salvation, rejection to destruction. There is a sobering historical precedent: even when prophets warned Israel again and again, many still refused to believe, and Paul uses this as a parallel.

Responses to the Sermon (vv. 42–52)

After the sermon, many followed Paul and Barnabas, wanting to hear more, and some were converted. Others — particularly the Jewish leaders — became jealous and hostile, eventually persecuting Paul and Barnabas and expelling them from the region. The Gentiles, by contrast, rejoiced and honored the word of God, and the message spread throughout the region. Paul and Barnabas shook the dust off their feet, following Jesus's instruction to his disciples: if rejected, do not debate or argue — simply leave. Despite the opposition and persecution, the gospel advanced and the first missionary journey bore significant fruit.

Application: Sharing the Gospel

  • If someone rejects the gospel after a clear presentation, do not enter into argument or philosophical debate — it is rarely productive.
  • The believer's responsibility is to sow and water; God is the one who causes belief. Some are not yet ready, and some may never respond.
  • Building a relationship and understanding a person's pain points are important before or alongside presenting the gospel.

Theological Q&A

Q1: Have Abraham and David been resurrected? David "sleeping" is a Jewish idiom for death — not literal unconsciousness. David and other Old Testament believers are with God now, but they have not yet been resurrected. Resurrection is a single future event at the end of time, coinciding with Jesus's second coming and the final judgment; it has not yet occurred for anyone except Jesus. Believers who have died are consciously with God — evidenced by Moses and Elijah appearing at the Transfiguration in full awareness — but this is distinct from resurrection. At the end there will be two resurrections, of the righteous and of the wicked, followed by judgment.

Q2: Resurrection vs. resuscitation — what about Lazarus and others? Resurrection is permanent transformation into a glorified, eternal body (like Jesus after Easter) — the person never dies again. Resuscitation is a return to the old physical body, still subject to death: Lazarus, Jairus's daughter, the widow's son, and the people raised at Jesus's crucifixion all died again later. Jesus is the "firstfruits" of resurrection precisely because every earlier raising from the dead was a resuscitation; had Lazarus truly been resurrected, Jesus could not be called the first.

Q3: How should Acts 13:39 be translated? A participant noticed that the Chinese translation in use was confusing and compared it with the NIV, which read more clearly. The resolution: the verse means that what Moses' law could not accomplish (justification), faith in Jesus does accomplish — the two translations convey the same truth with different phrasing. The NIV rendering ("everyone who believes is justified from everything you could not be justified from by the law of Moses") was found clearer. The key takeaway: the law proves guilt; faith in Christ justifies — this is the essence of grace.

  • Reflection: Do I present the gospel as the fulfillment of what God promised — or as a set of rules? Am I trusting in Christ alone for justification?
  • Reflection: Have I made my own decision about the gospel, rather than merely hearing it?
  • Practice: When the gospel is rejected, sow gently, pray, and leave the result to God instead of arguing.
