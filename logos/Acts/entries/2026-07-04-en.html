Acts 8:1-17 — Scattered to Samaria: Philip, Simon & the Spirit

Those who had been scattered preached the word wherever they went. Acts 8:4

Acts 8 marks a major structural turning point — chapters 1–7 centered on Jerusalem; 8–12 move into Judea and Samaria; 13 onward reach to the ends of the earth. Stephen's martyrdom triggers persecution that scatters believers, fulfilling Jesus' own promise in Acts 1: from human eyes a disaster, from God's a providential next step in his plan.

Saul's Approval of Stephen's Death

Saul, present at the stoning, didn't merely watch — the Greek word for his "approval" implies full agreement and active consent, not passive bystanding. Highly educated under Gamaliel and zealous for the Law, Saul genuinely believed he was defending God while persecuting Christians — a pattern Jesus had already foretold in John 16:2. The lesson: religious zeal and sincerity are not the same as godliness — devout, educated people can be sincerely wrong.

Philip's Mission to Samaria

Samaritans were despised by Jews as racially impure, with their own worship site and their own version of Scripture — Jews would detour entirely around Samaria rather than travel through it. Philip broke that taboo, going directly in to preach, echoing Jesus' own engagement with the Samaritan woman in John 4. He preached Christ first; miracles followed to confirm the message, not the reverse — the Samaritans "listened with one accord" and experienced "great joy," rooted in salvation, not spectacle.

Simon the Sorcerer

Simon had long amazed Samaria through sorcery, considered "the Great Power of God" — power that in fact came from Satan, not God. He too believed and was baptized under Philip's ministry, marveling at the miracles. The key distinction: Philip pointed to Christ and sought God's glory; Simon sought his own — a test that still applies to spiritual leaders today. Whether Simon's faith was genuine remains an open question the group left for the following session; a true conversion would mean he could no longer draw on sorcery, since no one can serve God while also drawing power from Satan.

Peter and John Sent to Samaria

Word that Samaritans had received God's word reached Jerusalem, and the apostles sent Peter and John — a historically significant act affirming that Samaritans were now full members of the body of Christ, not a minor administrative visit. John's presence is especially striking: this was the same John who once asked Jesus to call down fire on a Samaritan village that rejected them — now he came instead to bless and pray for Samaritans, evidence of genuine inner renewal by the Spirit, consistent with the emphasis on love running through his own later letters. The apostles laid hands on them and prayed, and the Samaritans received the Holy Spirit — even though they had already believed and been baptized.

Two Works of the Spirit

This raised the session's core theological question: if the Samaritans had already believed and been baptized, why had the Spirit "not yet fallen on any of them"? Three related but distinct things are in view: the Spirit's inward work in conversion — invisible, internal, the thing that makes belief possible at all, and true of every believer; Paul's own use of "baptism of the Holy Spirit" to mean this same inward, regenerating union with Christ through faith; and the outward "pouring out" or Pentecostal empowerment Jesus describes in Acts 1 — a distinct, visible, tangible experience, as at Pentecost, and repeated at other points in Acts. The Samaritans were already saved before the apostles arrived; what they received through the laying on of hands was the outward, visible kind — externally noticeable (Simon could see it happening), functioning as a divine seal confirming their full acceptance into God's people. The Samaritan woman's own conversion in John 4 offers a parallel: genuinely saved, with no outward Pentecostal sign attached. Some traditions wrongly require a visible sign as proof of salvation — Scripture's own standard is simply to "confess with your mouth and believe in your heart" (Romans 10); believers can and should still seek ongoing filling by the Spirit (Ephesians 5:18), a pursuit distinct from the moment of salvation itself.

  • Application: Philip and Stephen were chosen as deacons for practical service, yet both preached the gospel powerfully — evangelism was never reserved for pastors or professionals, and the early church ran on ordinary believers acting as witnesses, not passive recipients of someone else's ministry.
