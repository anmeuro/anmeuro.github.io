Acts 14:21–15:12 — Return to Antioch and the Jerusalem Council Dispute: Salvation by Grace Alone

We believe it is through the grace of our Lord Jesus that we are saved, just as they are. Acts 15:11

The group studied the conclusion of Paul and Barnabas's first missionary journey (Acts 14:21–28) and the opening of the Jerusalem Council dispute (Acts 15:1–12). The central theological issue examined was whether Gentiles must be circumcised and observe Mosaic Law to be saved — a question Paul and Barnabas sharply rejected. The session affirmed that salvation comes by grace through faith alone, applicable equally to Jews and Gentiles, and drew parallels to doctrinal errors that persist in churches today.

Decisions Made

  • Salvation is by grace through faith alone, not by circumcision or observance of Mosaic Law — this was affirmed as the non-negotiable doctrinal position of the group.
  • Core Christian doctrines identified as non-negotiable and not subject to compromise: (1) justification by faith, (2) the Trinity, and (3) the full divinity and full humanity of Jesus Christ.
  • Baptism is a symbol of salvation, not its cause — drawing a direct parallel to the circumcision debate, the group agreed that requiring baptism for salvation reflects the same category of error.
  • Church practices (attendance, worship, tithing) must be voluntary, rooted in gratitude, not imposed as obligatory conditions for being a "true Christian."

Passage 1 — Acts 14:21–28: Completion of the First Missionary Journey

Paul and Barnabas's Return Route

After preaching in Derbe and making many disciples, Paul and Barnabas retraced their steps through every city where they had previously been persecuted — Lystra, Iconium, and Antioch in Pisidia — rather than taking the shorter direct route back to Syrian Antioch. The deliberate choice to return via the longer, dangerous route demonstrated that church planting alone was insufficient; discipleship, strengthening believers, and appointing leadership were equally essential components of mission.

Two Purposes of the Return Visit

  • Encouragement and strengthening: Paul exhorted believers that "we must go through many hardships to enter the kingdom of God" — a teaching consistent with Jesus's own warnings and Paul's later letters.
  • Appointing elders: In each church, Paul and Barnabas selected and ordained local leaders to provide ongoing pastoral care and protection for new believers.

Theology of Suffering

Paul's teaching on hardship was carefully distinguished from ascetic or merit-based suffering found in religions such as Tibetan Buddhism or Hinduism, where self-inflicted pain is pursued as a path to enlightenment or reward. The Christian position: suffering is inevitable but not salvific — enduring hardship builds character and draws believers closer to God, but salvation comes solely by God's grace. Paul's formulation from Romans was cited: "suffering produces perseverance, perseverance produces character" — suffering transforms believers but does not earn salvation.

Report to Antioch

Upon returning to Syrian Antioch, Paul and Barnabas reported to the congregation what God had done through them, deliberately framing all outcomes as God's work rather than their own achievement. They specifically highlighted how God had opened the door of faith to the Gentiles — a historically significant milestone recognized as the first officially Gentile-targeted missionary journey.

Passage 2 — Acts 15:1–12: The Jerusalem Council Dispute

The Presenting Controversy

Certain Jewish Christians from Judea arrived in Antioch teaching that Gentiles must be circumcised and keep the Law of Moses to be saved — a position Paul and Barnabas disputed "sharply," not as a minor disagreement but as a fundamental doctrinal error. The group noted the irony that some of these insistent teachers were former Pharisees who had become believers — their conversion was remarkable, but their attachment to tradition made them resistant to the gospel's implications for Gentiles.

What Circumcision Actually Signifies

Circumcision was explained as a sign of God's covenant with Abraham, not a condition of righteousness: Abraham was declared righteous by God before he was circumcised — justified by faith, with circumcision serving as a subsequent symbol to remind his descendants of their covenant identity. The sign's purpose was to help Israel remember they belonged to God, not to the world — it was an identity marker, not a salvific act.

Peter's Testimony (Acts 15:6–12)

Peter reminded the council of the conversion of Cornelius (Acts 10) — a Roman centurion and Gentile who received the Holy Spirit before circumcision, along with his entire household. Peter's argument: God made no distinction between Jews and Gentiles, purifying hearts by faith in both cases; the same grace that saved Jewish believers saved Gentile believers. Peter challenged the circumcision party with a pointed question: "Why do you put on the disciples a yoke that neither we nor our ancestors have been able to bear?" — acknowledging that even Jewish believers had never perfectly kept the Mosaic Law.

Two Groups in the Early Church

The Jerusalem gathering revealed a clear division among early believers:

  • Group 1 (Circumcision party): Believed in Jesus but insisted Gentiles must also be circumcised and observe Mosaic Law to be truly saved.
  • Group 2 (Paul, Barnabas, Peter): Held that salvation comes through faith and grace alone, with no ethnic or ritual preconditions.

Contemporary Applications Drawn from the Text

Circumcision → Baptism Parallel

The error of the circumcision party has a modern equivalent in churches that teach baptism is required for salvation — treating a symbol as the mechanism of salvation rather than its celebration. Baptism is important and meaningful, but it is a public declaration and celebration of salvation already received, not the act that secures it.

Imposed Religious Obligations

The "heavy yoke" Peter condemned finds modern parallels in churches that require mandatory attendance, specific worship behaviors, or tithing as proof of genuine faith. These practices are valuable when done from a heart of gratitude, but become spiritually harmful when framed as obligatory conditions for being accepted by God or recognized as a Christian.

Non-Negotiable Core Doctrines

Three doctrines were identified as pillars of Christianity that cannot be compromised without departing from the faith:

  • Justification by faith alone (not by works, ritual, or ethnic identity).
  • The Trinity — one God in three persons (Father, Son, Holy Spirit); groups denying this are not authentically Christian.
  • The full divinity and full humanity of Jesus — 100% God and 100% human; reducing Jesus to merely a teacher, prophet, or good man is a departure from orthodoxy.
