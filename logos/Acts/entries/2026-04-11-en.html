Acts 3 — Healing at the Beautiful Gate & Peter's Sermon

Silver and gold I do not have, but what I do have I give you. In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, walk! Acts 3:6

After finishing Acts 2 — Pentecost and Peter's sermon, with about 3,000 believers added — the group moved into Acts 3: Peter and John healing a lame beggar at the temple's Beautiful Gate, and Peter's sermon that follows.

Old Covenant Sacrifice vs. Jesus' Redemption

Old Testament sacrifices had to be repeated daily, only ever covering sin temporarily rather than resolving it — foreshadowing the need for a more perfect sacrifice. Peter and John went up to the temple to pray at three in the afternoon (the ninth hour, Jewish time) — the very hour of both temple sacrifice and prayer, and the same hour Jesus breathed his last on the cross. As God's own Lamb, Jesus' death at that hour resolved the problem of sin "once for all," ending the need for animal sacrifice. Early believers, though they understood the temple sacrifices were no longer necessary, still kept the custom of temple prayer for a time — a transitional period between covenants, which also gave the disciples a natural way to reach people and preach the gospel at the temple.

The Symbolism of the Beggar at the Beautiful Gate

This man, lame from birth and disabled for 40 years, was carried to the Beautiful Gate every day to beg — everyone entering the temple to pray knew him. The gate itself, magnificent and adorned with gold and silver, stood in stark contrast to the ragged, destitute beggar sitting beneath it. That contrast carries meaning: before knowing God, every person is as destitute as this beggar in the face of God's glory. The gate represents an outwardly beautiful religious system that has no power in itself to save — the beggar sat before it daily, unable to enter and unable to be healed. As Jesus said, "I am the gate for the sheep" (John's Gospel) — life and redemption come only through Jesus as the true gate, not through external religious ritual.

Eye Contact Before the Healing

Before healing him, Peter and John asked the beggar to look at them — to surface his real longing and need. Jesus himself, in every healing, asked "What do you want me to do for you?" — not because he didn't already know, but so the person would voice their own helplessness, longing, and faith. Prayer is not a matter of luck: God wants us to keep asking and knocking, and in that process our posture shifts from careless to earnest, from relying on ourselves — credentials, background, money, connections — to relying on God alone. Through prayer God draws us into deeper, more intimate relationship, and we practice patience and faith, coming to see that the answer rests with God, not with us.

Peter's Transformation

The old Peter relied on his own strength — drawing a sword to cut off the high priest's servant's ear when Jesus was arrested — and proudly declared he would never deny his Lord. The Spirit-filled Peter is utterly changed, relying on neither ability, method, nor background: "Silver and gold I do not have," he says, meaning he relies on God's power alone. His declaration is precise — "in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth" specifies not just anyone named Jesus (a common name at the time) but that particular Jesus of Nazareth who was crucified and rose from the dead; "Christ" names him as the Anointed One, the Messiah God had promised.

Peter's Sharp Rebuke of the Crowd

Peter's sermon to the crowd is deliberately sharp, meant to cut to the heart. He calls Jesus the "Holy and Righteous One" — a title used over 40 times in the Old Testament for God alone — effectively saying, "you crucified God." Though Pilate wanted to release Jesus, the crowd forced his hand and demanded the release of the murderer Barabbas instead. Jesus is the "Author of Life," and it is not possible to kill the source of life itself — so God raised him from the dead; death could never hold him.

A Teaching Question: Why Didn't Jesus Heal This Man Earlier?

This beggar had sat at the Beautiful Gate since childhood; Jesus must have passed him many times entering the temple — so why wasn't he healed then? The answer is God's timing: not neglect, but that "it was not yet God's time." Jesus lived in constant communion and prayer with the Father, knowing the Father's timing. When Peter and John healed him — in front of the crowd, at the hour of prayer, at the temple — the miracle could testify to Jesus' resurrection, glorify God, and bring many more to salvation. This was God's better, more perfect plan.

Praying "In the Name of Jesus Christ"

Prayer should close with "in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ" — a phrase carrying two layers of meaning. First, his authority and power: we pray under his authority, trusting the outcome to him, not relying on our own authority or ability. Second, identification with God's character: our prayers should align with God's love, kindness, mercy, justice, and holiness, handing the outcome to God and trusting that he knows what is truly best — even though our own prayers are often shortsighted, focused only on material things. This is not a mechanical formula: Peter emphasizes not just "the name" but "faith in his name" — faith is the substance, not a phrase repeated by rote, and faith itself is a gift from God, not something we can take credit for. We can pray, as the man in the Gospels did, "I believe; help my unbelief."

  • Next week: continuing through the rest of Acts 3 (from verse 17 on).
  • Practice: close personal prayer with "in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ," reflecting on both its layers of meaning — authority, and alignment with God's character.
  • Reflect: am I relying too much on my own ability, background, or connections in prayer, rather than on God alone? Am I patient to wait for God's timing?
  • Open question: why can't miracles alone change a heart? Israel witnessed countless miracles and still did not believe; the disciples followed Jesus for three years, saw miracle after miracle, and still fled when he was arrested. Only the Word of God and the Spirit's work can truly transform and save a person. Peter's sermon here is held up as a model gospel witness — entirely centered on Jesus, with no mention of himself; in our own witness, the focus should rest on who Jesus is and what God has done, not on ourselves.
