Acts 2 — Peter's Pentecost Sermon & the Early Church's Shared Life

All the believers were together and had everything in common. Acts 2:44

This session went deeper into Peter's Pentecost sermon — his use of the Psalms, the contrast with Moses at Sinai — before turning to how the newly formed church actually lived together.

Peter's Use of the Psalms

Peter quoted David's Psalms to establish Jesus' divine identity, teaching that Christ is not merely a descendant of David but the true Son of God. This connects to Jesus' own words in John's Gospel, "Before Abraham was, I am" — the present-tense "I am" echoing God's own name, "I AM WHO I AM," and pointing to Jesus' eternal existence and divinity. Peter's reference to the "footstool" psalm likewise points to Jesus' royal status and his victory over Satan and his enemies through the resurrection.

What Actually Changes Hearts

Speaking in tongues or crying out does not itself change a heart — what genuinely moves people is the Word of God, understood. Paul's instruction was for believers to stop speaking in tongues when unbelievers are present without interpretation, since incomprehensible tongues only confuse; what matters is gospel explanation people can actually understand. Peter's own transformation traces this arc — from reaching for a physical sword to wielding the sword of the Spirit, God's Word, moving from external force to being filled with the Spirit's inward power.

Sinai and Pentecost

The session drew out a striking parallel between Moses receiving the Ten Commandments at Sinai and the later Pentecost — both marked by God's descent and the Spirit's appearance, yet Pentecost begins the New Covenant, not a renewal of the Law. At the Old Testament Pentecost, the golden calf incident led to 3,000 deaths; at the New Testament Pentecost, 3,000 people repented and believed. That contrast highlights how the New Covenant breaks the curse carried by the Old, and the mighty, redemptive work of the Spirit.

Life Together in the Early Church

Most of the 3,000 new believers were visitors in Jerusalem for the festival, planning only a brief stay. Moved by the Spirit, they chose to remain and keep learning from the apostles. Existing believers voluntarily sold property and possessions to support these travelers, distributing according to need. This sharing was voluntary — no one was forced to sell anything; temporary — a response to a specific situation, not a permanent church institution; and motivated by love, a genuine expression of mutual care. Later, when the Jerusalem church itself faced famine, those who had once received help returned the support — showing this loving relationship ran both ways.
