Acts 8:18-35 — Simon's Error & Philip and the Ethiopian Eunuch

How can I understand unless someone explains it to me? Acts 8:31

Two narratives in one session: Simon the Sorcerer's misguided attempt to buy spiritual power, and Philip's obedient detour to evangelize an Ethiopian official — together a study in what genuine faith looks like versus faith chasing power or signs.

Simon's Two Errors

Simon believed spiritual power could be possessed, acquired, or bought — when in truth spiritual gifts are freely given by grace, with God as the sovereign initiator. He also misunderstood the Holy Spirit as a power to wield over others rather than a divine person, the third member of the Trinity, to be worshipped and known. A genuinely converted believer asks how to grow in faith or serve the church; Simon wanted the ability itself, revealing where his heart actually was. His name gave rise to the term "simony" — buying or selling spiritual office — and the same error recurs throughout church history, not just among new believers, wherever the Spirit gets treated as a source of power and prosperity rather than God himself, as in today's prosperity gospel.

Peter's Response

Peter rebuked Simon sharply — "full of bitterness and captive to sin," a diagnosis that applies broadly, since every believer wrestles with the flesh — but also offered a path forward: repent and seek God's forgiveness. Notably, Simon asked the apostles to pray for him rather than personally repenting before God — a subtle but real distinction. Others can and should pray for us, but no one can obtain forgiveness on our behalf; God is more pleased by a direct, personal confession than by relying on someone else's prayer, however weak that confession might be.

Philip's Obedience

After real revival in Samaria — healings, conversions, great joy — an angel told Philip, with no explanation, to leave for a desert road toward Gaza. From a human perspective, leaving a thriving city ministry for an empty road made no sense; Philip obeyed immediately, without argument or negotiation. God's measure of success isn't crowd size — one person's salvation matters just as much in his eyes.

The Ethiopian Eunuch

A high-ranking official — effectively a finance minister — under the Ethiopian queen Candace, highly educated, reading the scroll of Isaiah in Hebrew on his way home from worshipping in Jerusalem. The distance and difficulty of that journey shows real spiritual hunger, and despite his education and status, he humbly admits he cannot understand what he's reading without guidance. Ethiopia's own connection to Israel goes back to the Queen of Sheba's visit to Solomon, which planted a tradition of faith in God that this official's own journey reflects — a heritage traceable to a Christian community in Ethiopia that has persisted for over a thousand years.

Isaiah 53: The "Forbidden Chapter"

The eunuch was reading Isaiah 53, the suffering servant passage — a detailed prophecy of Jesus' birth, ministry, suffering, and redemptive death, written some 700 years before his birth and before Rome had even invented crucifixion, yet describing it precisely. In Judaism today this chapter is often skipped in synagogue readings, since it applies too directly to Jesus as Messiah; common interpretations instead read it as Isaiah writing about himself, or as describing Israel's collective suffering, since many Jews cannot accept a suffering Messiah, expecting instead a conquering king who restores national glory (Islam, similarly, honors Jesus as a prophet while denying he is Messiah or Son of God, for much the same reason). The eunuch's own question — who is the prophet talking about? — opened the door for Philip to explain the full gospel.

Jesus' Silence, Prophecy Fulfilled

Isaiah 53 describes the servant silent before his accusers, "like a sheep to the slaughter." Jesus' silence at trial was not weakness but willing submission to God's plan, deliberately fulfilling Isaiah's words — Pilate himself found no grounds for a charge, yet Jesus accepted the unjust verdict, bearing shame so sinners could receive righteousness. Even his own disciples, who had seen the Transfiguration, fled at the crucifixion — no one at the time fully understood what was happening.

  • Prayer focus: for Jewish and Muslim friends to be freed from preconceptions about the Messiah and to meet the true Jesus — humble, born in a manger, crucified as a criminal, yet the fulfillment of all prophecy. A growing number of Jewish people are coming to faith in Jesus through Isaiah 53, though still a minority.
