Acts 3:17-26 — The Prophesied Messiah & the Call to Repent

Repent, then, and turn to God, so that your sins may be wiped out, that times of refreshing may come from the Lord. Acts 3:19

After healing the lame man, Peter tells the crowd that Jesus is the prophesied Messiah whom they crucified but who rose from death. Despite their role in his death, Peter calls them "brothers" and urges repentance, promising forgiveness and refreshing — truth about sin delivered together with compassion.

Love and Justice Together

Peter names the crowd's sin plainly, yet addresses them as brothers, without hatred despite their role as Christ's enemies — the same pattern Paul shows elsewhere, loving people deeply while being severe about sin when needed. Love is not blind tolerance or covering up wrongdoing; genuine love operates alongside righteousness, willing to point out what is wrong.

Understanding Repentance

Repentance is not merely feeling sorry — it requires turning from sin and real life change, not regret or repeated apology without transformed behavior. True repentance requires recognizing one's sin and a genuine conviction from the heart that it is wrong. That conviction comes from the Holy Spirit, and differs from condemnation: condemnation brings guilt, shame, and hopelessness, while the Spirit's conviction brings assurance, hope, and desire to change.

The Benefits of Repentance

God himself wipes out and forgives sin. Times of refreshing follow — peace, comfort, strength, healing, and spiritual blessing, with boldness to approach God without fear. Peter extends the promise beyond individual salvation to the whole nation of Israel, pointing to a final restoration when Christ returns.

Jesus as the Prophesied Messiah

Moses himself predicted that God would raise up "a prophet like me" — a striking comparison of the coming Messiah to Moses. First-century Jews had confused expectations, looking for three separate figures — the Messiah, a prophet like Moses, and a returning Elijah — without realizing all three point to the same person: Jesus.

Moses as a Type of Christ

Moses mediated the old covenant between Israel and God; Jesus mediates the new covenant between sinful humanity and God. Moses delivered Israel from Egypt through the first Passover; Jesus delivers humanity from sin as the true Passover Lamb. Moses received the law at Sinai and gave it to Israel; Jesus fulfilled and completed what the law required. Joseph, too, prefigures Christ — betrayed by his own brothers, yet used by God to save many.

God's Sovereignty Over Evil

The crucifixion, the most horrific evil imaginable, was still part of God's plan foretold by the prophets — echoing Romans 8:28, that all things, good and bad, work together for good for those who love God. Even so, Peter is clear that the crowd's ignorance in rejecting Jesus does not excuse the sin or exempt them from judgment.

Prophetic Continuity

Every prophet from Samuel onward spoke of the Messiah's coming and the coming day of judgment. God's promise to Abraham — that all peoples on earth would be blessed through his offspring — pointed to Jesus, who came first for the Jews and then for all humanity. Peter draws a single line from Abraham, through Moses, through the prophets, all pointing to Christ.

Peter's Transformation

Peter — once an uneducated fisherman with no formal training in Scripture — speaks here with real insight into the Old Testament, entirely because of the Holy Spirit's renewing work. Jesus promised his followers would do even greater works than he did; that promise rests on faith, freedom from sin, humility, prayer, and being filled with the Spirit.
